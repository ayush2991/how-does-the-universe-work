Part 01 · Special Relativity

Why Motion Through Space Affects Time

A curiosity-driven exploration of why moving through space naturally alters our experience of time.

By Aayush Agarwal • Interactive Explorable • 10 min read

When many of us first encounter Albert Einstein’s Special Theory of Relativity, it often arrives wrapped in abstract thought experiments: high-speed trains flashing light pulses at platforms, mirrors bouncing photons up and down, and algebraic formulas filled with square roots.

While these classic explanations are mathematically rigorous, they can sometimes leave a curious reader feeling ungrounded. We might follow the steps on paper, but we are still left wondering: Why should moving through space have anything to do with how fast a clock ticks? What is the underlying physical intuition that connects space and time?

Our goal in this series is to set aside the abstract formulas and build an intuitive understanding from the ground up. By starting with a familiar scenario that anyone can visualize on a flat surface, we can follow a natural chain of geometric reasoning that leads directly to the core insights of relativity: why moving clocks run slow, why the speed of light is a natural cosmic limit, and how we all experience spacetime in our daily lives.

Let’s begin with a simple observation on a flat plane.

1. Starting with Simple Geometry: Two Cars on a 2D Plane

Imagine a broad, flat open field. We draw a standard coordinate grid on the ground with two perpendicular directions: East (which we will call x₁) and North (which we will call x₂).

At the starting point (0,0), we place two cars: a Blue Car and an Orange Car.

Both drivers turn on cruise control, locking their speed to the exact same value: 60 miles per hour (V = 60 mph). Neither vehicle can speed up, and neither can slow down.

Now they begin driving:

1. The Blue Car points its wheels due North along the x₂ axis. Because all of its motion is directed North, its Northward speed is 60 mph, and its Eastward speed is 0 mph.
2. The Orange Car chooses a different path. It angles its heading 60° toward the East (away from North) along a diagonal.

Consider a simple question: After driving for an hour, will the Orange Car be at the same Northward position as the Blue Car?

Intuitively, we know it will not. Because the Orange Car angled toward the East, its fixed 60 mph speed is now shared between two perpendicular directions. We can see exactly how that speed is split by looking at the right triangle formed by its heading:

• Speed Eastward (x₁): Scales with the sine of the angle:
V_East = V × sin(60°) = 60 × (√3 / 2) ≈ 60 × 0.866 ≈ 52 mph

• Speed Northward (x₂): Scales with the cosine of the angle:
V_North = V × cos(60°) = 60 × 0.5 = 30 mph

Notice where that 52 mph comes from: because sin(60°) ≈ 0.866, the car directs about 86.6% of its speed toward the East. And because cos(60°) = 0.5, its progress Northward is cut exactly in half, down to 30 mph.

2. Treating Time as an Axis

Now, let us take this geometric idea and make a simple shift in how we picture the world.

Suppose we draw another 2D grid. We will keep the horizontal axis as Space (x), representing motion to the left or right.

For the vertical axis, instead of labeling it "North," let’s label it Time (t).

What does it mean to move along this vertical axis?

Consider yourself sitting in a chair right now. You may be completely motionless relative to your room; your speed through space is zero.

Yet, are you standing still in time?

Clearly not. Each second that passes on your watch carries you steadily forward into the future. Even when you are stationary in space, you are constantly progressing along the Time axis.

3. The Thought Experiment: A Fixed Speed Through Spacetime

Now that we have a plane with Space on one axis and Time on the other, let us pose a hypothetical question:

What if nature operated under a single, elegant geometric rule—namely, that every object in existence moves through this combined Space-Time plane at a single, constant total speed, V?

Let’s trace what such a rule would require:

• An Observer at Rest: If an observer sits completely still in space, their spatial velocity is zero (v_space = 0). Since their total speed vector has a constant length V, the entire vector must point straight up along the Time axis: v_time = V. All of their motion is directed through time, and their clock ticks forward at its full, normal rate.

• An Observer Moving Through Space: Now, suppose this person begins moving across space at a speed v_space. Look at what happens as that vector tilts into the space dimension:

Just like the car in our first example, the traveler cannot create extra speed. If part of their fixed speed vector is dedicated to moving through Space, that motion must be drawn from their motion through Time:

The Spacetime Trade-Off: v_time = √( V² - v_space² )
Under this rule, the moment an object begins moving through space, its rate of progress through time must decrease.

4. Connecting to the Real World: The Cosmic Constant c

This thought experiment might seem like a neat mathematical curiosity. But what makes it so fascinating is that this is remarkably close to how our physical universe actually works.

When Albert Einstein formulated Special Relativity in 1905, and Hermann Minkowski subsequently revealed its geometric structure, they showed that space and time are fundamentally woven into a single four-dimensional continuum: spacetime.

And what is that constant speed V that governs motion through spacetime?

It is the constant denoted by c—commonly known as the speed of light (approximately 299,792 km/s, or 671 million miles per hour).

In this geometric view:

• When you are sitting at rest in your chair, your motion is directed almost entirely through time at speed c. Your watch ticks at its standard rate.
• If a traveler boards a spacecraft and travels through space at a significant fraction of light speed—say, 86.6% of c (v_space = 0.866c, directly mirroring the sin(60°) angle from our car example)—they are directing a substantial portion of their spacetime velocity into space.

How much of their motion remains along the time dimension?

v_time = √( c² - v_space² ) = √( c² - (0.866c)² ) = √( 0.25c² ) = 0.50c

Their progress through time is cut exactly in half!

This effect is known as Time Dilation. It is not a mechanical defect in clocks or an optical trick of delayed signals; it is a direct consequence of how motion is distributed between space and time.

5. Natural Consequences: The Speed Limit and Timeless Light

Once we view spacetime through this geometric lens, several famous features of relativity emerge naturally from the geometry.

Why is the Speed of Light an Unbreakable Limit?

It is often stated that nothing can exceed the speed of light. People often wonder: Why? Could a sufficiently advanced propulsion system eventually push an object past this barrier?

Consider what happens if an object directs 100% of its spacetime speed into space:

If an object travels through space at v_space = c, how much speed remains for time?

v_time = √( c² - c² ) = 0
Its motion through time drops to zero. A photon experiences zero elapsed time across the entire cosmos.

Just as a car on a 2D plane cannot travel "more than 100% East," an object cannot divert more than all of its speed into space. To exceed c would require having more total motion than nature provides. The speed of light is not an arbitrary barrier; it is simply the full magnitude of motion available in spacetime.

Why Don’t We Notice This in Daily Life?

If moving through space reduces our progress through time, why doesn't our watch fall behind when we take a long drive or an airplane flight?

The reason comes down to the immense magnitude of c. Light travels at roughly 1,080,000,000 km/h. When you sit in a commercial airplane cruising at 900 km/h, your speed through space as a fraction of light speed is approximately 0.00000083.

The reduction in your motion through time is less than a few nanoseconds over an entire flight. Human life takes place in an extreme low-speed environment compared to the speed of light. Because all of us are moving through space at nearly zero compared to c, we all share almost exactly the same rate of passage through time.

6. Curious Questions: What Can We Actually Do With This?

Once this geometric picture clicks, it naturally stirs up questions about how time behaves in extreme situations. Let’s explore two fascinating scenarios that physics allows us to contemplate:

Can You Speed Up or Slow Down Time?

If motion through space trades off against motion through time, can you use this to your advantage?

• How to slow down your own time (and leap into the future): If you want to travel into Earth's distant future, the recipe is straightforward in theory: build a spacecraft capable of traveling at 99.99% of c. Because nearly all of your spacetime velocity is dedicated to moving through space, your progress through time slows to a crawl relative to people on Earth. For every day that passes for you aboard the ship, roughly 70 days elapse on Earth. Spend a year cruising the cosmos at this speed, and when you return, you will have aged just one year while Earth has advanced by 70 years. You cannot travel backward into the past, but one-way travel into the future is a built-in feature of our universe.

• Can you speed up your time? Looking back at our diagram, an interesting limitation reveals itself: you can never travel through time faster than c. When sitting completely at rest in space, you are already pointing 100% of your vector into time (v_time = c). Moving in any direction in space can only reduce your speed through time, never increase it. In flat spacetime, you are already experiencing time at the maximum speed nature permits.

Nature's Built-in Proof: Atmospheric Muons

Nature even provides a direct, observable demonstration of this geometry right above our heads.

When high-energy cosmic rays from deep space strike the upper atmosphere—about 10 kilometers above the ground—they create unstable subatomic particles called muons.

In laboratory experiments, a muon decays in just 2.2 millionths of a second (2.2 microseconds). Even if a muon traveled at nearly the speed of light, simple arithmetic suggests it could travel at most about 660 meters before vanishing:

Distance = Speed × Lifetime ≈ (300,000 km/s) × (0.0000022 s) ≈ 0.66 km (660 meters)
Starting 10 km up, virtually zero muons should ever survive to reach the ground.

Yet, particle detectors on the Earth's surface detect millions of muons every single day. Why? Test it for yourself below:

7. Expanding Our View: Spacetime with Two Dimensions of Space (x₁, x₂) and Time (t)

Up to this point, our spacetime diagrams simplified space down to a single horizontal line (x). This was deliberate: it allowed us to clearly see the fundamental trade-off between moving through space and moving through time on a flat page.

In reality, of course, space is not a 1D track. We can walk East, run North, or travel diagonally in any direction we please. How does nature calculate time dilation when you move simultaneously in multiple spatial directions?

Steering in Multiple Directions: The Pythagorean Extension

Notice how naturally this connects back to our very first thought experiment in Section 1. There, we had two cars traveling on a two-dimensional spatial field: x₁ (East) and x₂ (North).

Whenever you travel diagonally on this ground plane, the Pythagorean theorem tells us your total speed across space is simply:

v_space = √( v_x1² + v_x2² )
Your total spatial speed is the hypotenuse of your motion East and your motion North.

Now, we plug this spatial hypotenuse directly into nature's cosmic speed equation. The total speed constraint through spacetime becomes:

( v_x1² + v_x2² ) + v_time² = c² ⟹ v_time = √( c² - v_x1² - v_x2² )
Motion across East (x₁) and North (x₂) both draw from the exact same finite reservoir of speed through time (t).

The 3D Geometry: From a Flat Arc to a Spherical Dome

In our earlier 2D diagrams, the cosmic speed limit formed a circular arc. When we add a second spatial dimension, that circular arc sweeps out a full 360 degrees around the vertical Time axis.

The result is a magnificent 3D spherical dome (hemisphere) of radius c, sitting right on top of the (x₁, x₂) ground plane:

Planting the Seed for Part 2: The Spacetime Loaf

Why take this step into three dimensions?

Our real universe has three dimensions of space (x, y, z) plus one dimension of time (t), making spacetime four-dimensional. But human perception is inherently three-dimensional—we cannot visualize four mutually perpendicular axes simultaneously.

By retaining two spatial dimensions (x₁, x₂) and one time dimension (t), we preserve the complete geometric behavior of relativity in a form that our eyes can directly explore.

Imagine stacking every instant of time—past, present, and future—vertically along the time axis. Spacetime forms an extended three-dimensional block: what physicist Brian Greene famously described as the Spacetime Loaf.

For an observer at rest, a single instant in time—the collection of all events that are happening right now—is simply a horizontal planar slice through this loaf (shown in cyan in the interactive 3D simulation above).

This reveals the captivating question that will guide our next essay:

If moving through space tilts your velocity vector through time, what happens to your slice of "Now"? Does a moving observer cut the spacetime loaf at the exact same horizontal angle as someone at rest, or does their slice of the universe tilt?

8. Looking Ahead: Questions for Part 2

By starting from ground-level geometry, we have built an intuitive, visual understanding:

1. Objects have a fixed total capacity for motion through spacetime, bounded by c.
2. Sitting at rest in space directs 100% of this motion along the vertical time axis.
3. Diverting motion into any spatial direction (x₁, x₂) naturally shrinks the time component, causing clocks to tick slower (time dilation).
4. When an object directs 100% of its motion into space (v = c), its vertical progress through time reaches zero—which is why photons experience no elapsed time.
5. Spacetime can be visualized as an extended block (a "loaf"), where a moment of time is a slice through that volume.

This leads directly into the famous paradoxes of Einstein's theory:

If all motion is relative, what happens when two spacecraft drift past each other in deep space?

From astronaut A’s point of view, astronaut B is moving, so B’s clock should be running slow. But from astronaut B’s perspective, A is moving, so A’s clock should be running slow.

Can both observers be correct? If they turn around and meet again, who is actually younger?

In Part 2, we will slice open the Spacetime Loaf to resolve the Twin Paradox, discover the Relativity of Simultaneity, and see why moving rulers must physically contract along their direction of motion.
