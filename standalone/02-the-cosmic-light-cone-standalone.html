Part 02 · Special Relativity

The Cosmic Light Cone: Mapping Space & Time

Bridging the speed-tradeoff into coordinate spacetime. Why 90° in velocity space becomes 45° in the light cone, and how cosmic events live inside or outside our human lifespan.

By Aayush Agarwal • Interactive Explorable • 10 min read

In Part 1, we discovered an extraordinary geometric principle: everyone and everything is moving through the combined fabric of spacetime at a single invariant speed: the speed of light, c.

When you sit motionless in your chair, 100% of your speed is devoted to moving forward through time. The moment you step into a car or rocket and begin moving through space, part of that total speed is diverted sideways. Because your total speed is fixed, your motion through time inevitably decreases—and your personal clock ticks more slowly.

To visualize this tradeoff, we plotted an intuitive coordinate system called Velocity Space:

  • The vertical axis represented speed through time (v_time).
  • The horizontal axis represented speed through space (v_space).
  • Your total speed vector was a needle of constant length c, tracing a quarter circle from straight up (θ = 0°, sitting at rest) to purely horizontal (θ = 90°, pure light).

That picture — the speed tradeoff, the quarter-circle, the time dilation — gives us a powerful grasp of how fast things are moving through time versus space. But it is a graph of speeds. It tells us how someone's velocity is partitioned, not where they actually are or where they are going.

The natural next question is: can we draw an actual map of the universe in space and time? Not a graph of speeds, but a coordinate record — a picture that shows, for every moment, where each traveller is. The path a traveller traces through such a map is called their worldline: a continuous thread through space and time recording every position at every moment of their journey.

1. Drawing the Map: Coordinate Spacetime

The map we will draw has position x on the horizontal axis and time on the vertical axis — measured in units of ct, the distance light travels in a given time, so that both axes share the same physical dimension. Let us plant two boundary markers before filling in the space between them.

The first boundary is Alice, who stays perfectly at rest at position x = 0. She never moves through space — every unit of her total cosmic velocity flows purely forward through time. Her worldline in this map is a straight vertical line: she climbs steadily upward through time while her position in space never budges. This is one extreme: zero spatial motion, maximum aging.

The other extreme is the photon. At speed angle θ = 90°, every last unit of its cosmic velocity has been assigned to space — nothing is left for time. Its worldline cannot be vertical, since that would mean standing still in space. But what angle does it actually make? Because we chose the vertical axis in units of ct, one unit upward in time is exactly one unit rightward in space. A photon covering one light-second of space in one light-second of time traces a 1:1 path — exactly 45°. This is the other extreme: maximum spatial speed, zero aging. Any worldline steeper than 45° from vertical would require moving faster than light — which Part 1 showed is impossible.

Between Alice's vertical worldline and the photon's 45° diagonal lies all of physics for massive objects. Bob is that general case. He is free to move at any speed from rest up to — but never reaching — c. His worldline will always sit somewhere in the wedge between the two extremes: tilting more toward the vertical when he moves slowly, tilting closer to 45° as he approaches light speed. Sweeping the Wedge

The two boundaries are now drawn: Alice's vertical line on one side, the photon's 45° diagonal on the other. Use the presets to sweep Bob through the entire space in between:

  • At Rest (θ = 0°): Bob's worldline is vertical — he coincides with the first boundary. He is doing exactly what Alice does: zero spatial motion, aging at full rate. The two worldlines climb side by side.
  • Sub-light Cruising (θ = 30°): Bob moves into the wedge. His worldline tilts to φ ≈ 26.6° — well inside the 45° limit. The tick marks on his worldline spread apart: he is aging at 86.6% of Alice's rate, so coordinate time must advance further for each second of his personal clock.
  • Ultra-Relativistic (θ = 60°): Bob pushes deep into the wedge, reaching v = 0.866c. His worldline tilts to φ ≈ 40.9° — close to the photon's boundary but still inside it. His clock runs at half speed (γ = 2.00). The 45° wall is near, but he cannot reach it.
  • The Photon (θ = 90°): Bob reaches the second boundary. His worldline coincides with the photon's 45° diagonal. Notice the tick marks: they have vanished entirely. The photon accumulates no proper time at all — for it, time stands completely still. The velocity-space angle θ = 90° does not produce a 90° worldline; it produces a 45° worldline with zero aging along it.

Why 45° Is the Cosmic Speed Limit in the Map

The 45° slope of the photon's worldline is a direct consequence of our unit choice: by measuring the time axis in units of ct (light-seconds, light-years), we guarantee that light always traces a 1:1 path — one unit of space for every unit of ct — which is exactly 45°.

This makes 45° the hard boundary of the map. Any worldline tilted beyond 45° from the vertical would represent an object covering more than one light-second of space in one light-second of time — moving faster than c. Part 1 showed us that no object with mass can ever reach c, let alone exceed it: the speed-circle forbids it. So in the coordinate spacetime map, the 45° photon worldline is the outermost boundary that any massive worldline can approach but never cross. It is the cosmic speed limit drawn directly into the geometry.

Every worldline for matter — Alice's vertical line, Bob's tilted line at any speed — must stay between the vertical and that 45° diagonal. The region beyond 45° is not just empty; it is physically unreachable.

2. The Expanding Ripple: Building the 3D Light Cone

What happens if you turn on a light bulb for a fraction of a second at a single point in space?

In three-dimensional space, the flash emits a spherical shell of photons spreading outward in all directions at speed c:

x² + y² + z² = (c · t)²
The spherical light shell spreading outward into space with velocity c.

To picture this, let us work in just two spatial dimensions — x and y — and forget about depth for now. The flash starts as a single point. One moment later it has become a circle. Two moments later, a bigger circle. The radius grows at exactly the speed of light, so at time t the circle has radius r = ct.

Each of those circles is a snapshot — a single moment frozen in time. Now imagine lifting each snapshot upward by the amount of time that has passed: t = 0 stays at the bottom, t = 1 rises one step, t = 2 rises two, and so on. Stack them all in sequence and the expanding circles trace the surface of a cone. That cone is the Future Light Cone.

Conversely, running time backwards reveals all light rays from the past that are converging toward you right now. This forms the Past Light Cone.

The Three Realms of Reality

The 45° boundary of the Light Cone divides all of existence into three mutually exclusive geometric realms:

  1. The Causal Future (Timelike Future): The region inside the upper cone (c²t² > x² + y² with t > 0). Any particle of matter moving at less than light speed (v < c) will always travel inside this cone. This is the only part of the future you can ever visit, influence, or communicate with.
  2. The Causal Past (Timelike Past): The region inside the lower cone (t < 0). This contains every event in cosmic history that could have sent a signal (light, gravity, or sound) to reach you right now. Your memories, your DNA, and the light entering your eyes all originate strictly from inside your past light cone.
  3. The "Elsewhere" (Spacelike Region): The vast territory outside the cone (c²t² < x² + y²). Because no signal, influence, or matter can travel faster than light, nothing that happens in your Elsewhere can affect you right now, and nothing you do right now can affect it. It is completely causally severed from your present instant.

3. What This Means Intuitively: The Horizon of Human Experience

To truly appreciate the light cone, we must leave abstract coordinates behind and consider what it means for living beings standing under the stars.

The 8-Minute Sun

The Sun is 150 million kilometers away. Light, traveling at 300,000 km/s, takes approximately 8 minutes and 20 seconds to cross that gulf.

Suppose that right now, at this exact second, an evil alien vaporizes the Sun. What happens on Earth?

Nothing.

The birds keep singing, solar panels keep generating electricity, and the Earth continues orbiting peacefully through empty space. Why? Because the vaporization event is currently in Earth's Elsewhere.

Not only can light not reach us faster than 8 minutes, but gravity itself travels at the exact same speed: c. For 500 seconds, the Sun's disappearance is physically nonexistent to our world. Only when the edge of the light cone sweeping outward from the Sun at 45° finally collides with Earth's timeline does our planet suddenly plunge into darkness and drift into interstellar space.

A Supernova vs An 80-Year Human Lifespan

Now consider the stars scattered across the Milky Way.

Imagine a child is born on Earth today (t = 0). Over their typical 80-year human lifespan, their worldline travels straight up through time from t = 0 to t = 80 years at Earth's position (d = 0).

Because light travels at exactly 1 light-year per year, the 45° boundary of their experience means that in 80 years, the furthest light ray that can ever reach their eyes from an event happening today is 80 light-years away.

Suppose several celestial events occur on the day this child is born:

  • Proxima Centauri (4.2 light-years away) releases a stellar flare. The light crosses space and enters the child's past light cone when they are 4.2 years old. They can look through a backyard telescope and see it.
  • Vega (25 light-years away) erupts. The light races toward Earth, arriving when the person celebrates their 25th birthday. It is well inside their lifetime light cone.
  • Betelgeuse (640 light-years away) collapses into a catastrophic supernova today. Its light will travel across space for 640 years. When the person is 20, 50, or 80 years old, the supernova's light is still hundreds of light-years away! Across that human's entire life from birth to death, that supernova remains permanently trapped in their Elsewhere. No person alive on Earth today will ever witness it, read a news headline about it, or be affected by it.

4. Unifying the Two Pictures: From Velocity to Spacetime

Throughout this journey, we have looked at motion through two different lenses.

In Part 1, we drew Velocity Space: a circle showing how an object partitions its fixed cosmic speed c between moving through space (v_x = c · sin θ) and moving through time (v_t = c · cos θ). In Part 2, we drew Coordinate Spacetime: an actual map of positions and timestamps where worldlines climb upward through time at tilt angle φ.

How does the angle of velocity θ on the speed circle create the geometric tilt φ of a worldline on the map?

The slope of a worldline is its rate of spatial progress per unit of coordinate time:

tan φ = Δx / cΔt = v_x / c = sin θ
The spatial component on the speed circle directly dictates the slope of the worldline in spacetime.

Taking the arctangent gives the fundamental bridge connecting both worlds:

tan φ = sin θ  ⟹  φ = arctan(sin θ)
The spacetime tilt angle φ is directly governed by the velocity-space angle θ.

Studying these four panels side by side clarifies three profound principles of relativistic motion:

  • Why 90° in Velocity Space becomes 45° in Spacetime: In Velocity Space, turning 90° points all motion into space (v_x = c, v_t = 0). On the coordinate map, moving at light speed covers 1 light-second of space in 1 light-second of time (tan φ = 1), creating an exact 45° diagonal.
  • Why Moving Clocks Slow Down Along the Worldline: The tick marks drawn along each worldline represent intervals of personal time (dτ). Because dτ = dt · √(1 − v²/c²) = dt · cos θ, the rate of aging directly matches the vertical time velocity v_t. As the worldline tilts toward 45°, personal time stretches out until, for light itself, cos 90° = 0 and no personal time ticks ever occur.
  • The Unreachable Elsewhere: A worldline tilted past 45° would require tan φ > 1 ⟹ sin θ > 1, which is mathematically impossible for the unit circle and physically impossible for the cosmos. Everything outside the 45° diagonal is causal Elsewhere.

5. Looking Ahead: Slicing the Loaf in Part 3

Now that we have built the coordinate map of spacetime and understood the 45° geometry of the Light Cone, we are ready to tackle relativity's most mind-bending puzzle:

If Bob is moving relative to Alice, his worldline is tilted at angle φ. What does Bob consider to be happening "right now"?

For Alice, "Now" is a flat horizontal slice across the spacetime map. But because the speed of light must be identical in both directions for Bob, Bob's slice of simultaneous space cannot be horizontal. It must tilt upward!
